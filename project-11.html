Context
The "diapers and beer" question, properly answered
The Data
Groceries_dataset.csv from Kaggle — 38,765 observations of grocery purchases by 3,898 members over time. Three columns: Member_number, Date, itemDescription. Each row is one item bought; transactions are reconstructed by grouping items purchased by the same member on the same date.
The Question
When a customer buys product A, what other products are they likely to buy in the same trip? This is the classic "diapers and beer" retail question — find product associations strong enough to inform store layout, bundle pricing, or recommendation engines.
My Approach
Used R's arules package with the Apriori algorithm. Reshaped the long-format CSV into a transaction list (each transaction = items bought together). Ran Apriori with thresholds (support 0.0005, confidence 0.2, minlen 2) tuned to surface niche-but-meaningful associations rather than only the obvious ones.
What I Took Away
Choosing thresholds is the analyst's judgement call. Higher support gives you common-sense pairs (bread + butter); much lower support reveals niche patterns that are operationally interesting but easy to over-claim. Reporting both support and confidence is mandatory — high confidence on low support means "rare but reliable when it happens."
Key concepts
Support, Confidence, and why both matter
Support
P(A ∩ B)
Fraction of transactions containing both items. Tells you how common the pattern is overall.
Confidence
P(B | A)
Given that A was bought, the probability B was also bought. Tells you how reliable the rule is.
Lift
P(B|A) / P(B)
How much more likely B is given A vs. the baseline. Lift > 1 = positive association.
Why support alone isn't enough
A rule like "whole milk → bread" might have high support (lots of people buy both) but low confidence (because milk buyers don't necessarily also buy bread — many buy just milk). High support + low confidence = pattern is common but the rule is weak. Conversely, low support + high confidence = the pattern is rare, but when it happens, it's nearly certain. The latter is often more useful for targeted retail action.
Step 1 — Reshape
From long-format CSV to a transaction list

The raw CSV has one row per item purchased — but Apriori needs transactions (sets of items bought together). The grouping key is (Member_number + Date): all items a member bought on a given date form one transaction.

library(tidyverse)
library(dplyr)
library(arules)

# Load raw transactions
Groceries_dataset <- read.csv("Groceries_dataset.csv")

head(Groceries_dataset)
#   Member_number       Date     itemDescription
# 1          1808 21-07-2015      tropical fruit
# 2          2552 05-01-2015          whole milk
# 3          2300 19-09-2015           pip fruit
# ...

length(unique(Groceries_dataset$Member_number))
# [1] 3898  (unique customers)

# Reshape: group items by (Member, Date) into baskets
transaction_data <- Groceries_dataset %>%
  group_by(Member_number, Date) %>%
  summarise(items = list(itemDescription), .groups = "drop")

# Cast to arules transactions object
transaction_list <- as(transaction_data$items, "transactions")
Step 2 — Apriori
Running the algorithm with carefully chosen thresholds

The Apriori algorithm works in two phases: first finds all frequent itemsets (item combinations meeting the support threshold), then generates association rules from them that meet the confidence threshold. The clever bit is the "apriori property" — if an itemset is infrequent, all its supersets are too, so the search space prunes drastically.

# Run Apriori with tuned thresholds:
#   supp = 0.0005  →  rule must appear in at least ~10 baskets
#   conf = 0.20    →  rule must hold >= 20% of the time when antecedent occurs
#   minlen = 2     →  rules must have at least 2 items (1 antecedent + 1 consequent)

rules <- apriori(
  transaction_list,
  parameter = list(supp = 0.0005, conf = 0.2, minlen = 2)
)

# Inspect the first two rules
inspect(head(rules, 2))

Two of the rules surfaced by the algorithm:

Rule 1
{artificial sweetener} → {whole milk}
Support: 0.00053 · Confidence: 27.58%
If a customer buys artificial sweetener, there's a 27.58% chance they also buy whole milk in the same trip.
Rule 2
{brandy} → {whole milk}
Support: 0.00086 · Confidence: 34.21%
If a customer buys brandy, there's a 34.21% chance they also buy whole milk in the same trip.
Interpretation
What the rules mean — and what they don't
01
Niche, not dominant. Both rules have very low support (under 0.001), meaning these baskets are rare across the 14,963 reconstructed transactions. The 27-34% confidence is high, but only conditional on the antecedent occurring. Niche patterns, not dominant trends — exactly the language the conclusion uses.
02
Whole milk dominates the consequent side. Whole milk is the most-purchased item in this dataset, so it appears as the consequent of many rules. This is partly the algorithm telling you the truth about the data: almost any niche product, when bought, is bought alongside whole milk.
03
Practical use. A retailer could use these rules for targeted bundling (artificial-sweetener buyers get a milk coupon) or shelf placement (place niche items near whole milk to capture the cross-purchase). The honest framing is that this is a tactical insight for a small share of trips — not a strategic shift.
04
Lift is the missing metric to report. A natural next step would be to filter rules by lift > 1.5 to surface the strongest associations regardless of consequent frequency. The current Quarto report stops at support + confidence; lift filtering is the obvious extension.
Why this matters
Connecting Apriori to operations & analytics
→
InventoryIQ connection. The same association-rule technique used here drives cross-sell recommendations in retail systems — a natural extension to my P/07 InventoryIQ tool. Apriori on a retailer's transaction history would suggest which SKUs to bundle.
→
Plant operations transfer. Replace "customer baskets" with "alarm groups" — Apriori finds which plant alarms co-occur in the same window. Same algorithm, very different domain. Used in real industrial control systems for root-cause clustering.
→
Honest scale. 38,765 observations sounds large, but 14,963 transactions / 3,898 customers = ~3.8 baskets/customer on average. That's modest data. Real retail mining runs against tens of millions of transactions — and the same R code would scale, with appropriate machine resources.
