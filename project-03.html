Context
The plant & the study
The Plant
Agni Steels Sponge Iron Division at Ingur, Perundurai (Erode District, Tamil Nadu). A 100 TPD coal-based rotary kiln DRI unit producing sponge iron captively for the company's billet and TMT bar operations. Vertically integrated: Sponge → SMS (Steel Melting Shop) → Rolling Mill.
My Role
Operations Management intern, attached primarily to the sponge iron unit. Daily walkthroughs with the shift team, conversations with operators and the shift in-charge, time spent in the control room observing SCADA screens, and short visits to the billet casting area and rolling mill.
My Approach
An observational case study — not a Six Sigma project with measured data. I mapped the sponge iron process flow, recorded the problems operators repeatedly mentioned, and applied Pareto + Fishbone frameworks to rank causes by frequency/impact.
What I Took Away
In a 100 TPD coal-based DRI plant, kiln accretion (ring formation) is the dominant operational constraint. Raw material consistency is the next-most-important lever. SCADA gives visibility but doesn't predict accretion — predictive analytics (ANN/CFD) is the obvious next frontier.
Process Flow
Coal-based DRI — the rotary kiln process

The actual production sequence in the sponge iron unit. Standard for a 100 TPD coal-based DRI plant, validated by what I observed on-site.

01
Raw material yard. Iron ore (hematite lumps, 5–18 mm, sourced from Karnataka and Kumba/South Africa) stockpiled alongside non-coking coal (premium South African and domestic). Dolomite as flux for desulphurisation. Reddish ore piles, dark coal piles, clearly separated.
02
Material handling & preparation. Conveyor belts, crushers and sizing screens proportion the ore-coal-flux mix. Quality of sizing here directly affects bed permeability and downstream metallization.
03
Rotary kiln — the main reactor. A long inclined rotating furnace (~42 m × 3 m typical for 100 TPD). Material flows counter-current to combustion gases. Two thermal zones: preheating (~800–1000 °C) → reduction (~1050–1150 °C). Reactions: C + CO₂ → 2CO, then Fe₂O₃ → FeO → Fe (metallic). Residence time 6–8 hours. Operators monitor kiln-shell temperature profile via thermocouples; >1150 °C is avoided to prevent ring formation.
04
Rotary cooler. Hot DRI from the kiln passes through an indirect water-cooled rotary cooler down to ~100–150 °C — low enough to prevent re-oxidation, safe enough for handling.
05
Product separation & screening. Magnetic separators pull the metallic sponge iron from char, ash, and dolochar by-products. Vibrating screens sort by size (lumps vs. fines). Char is collected for resale or reuse.
06
Storage & dispatch. Sponge iron lumps and fines stored in silos/bunkers, dispatched to the captive Steel Melting Shop (SMS). Separately: an Electrostatic Precipitator (ESP) handles kiln exhaust dust for emission control — this is environmental compliance, not a production stage.
Field Observations
What I actually saw & heard

No measured downtime data was logged for this study. Observations below are from direct walkthroughs of the sponge iron unit and conversations with operators, shift in-charges and process engineers over multiple shifts.

01
Kiln accretion / ring formation was the recurring issue. On at least two days during my time on-site, the rotary kiln had to be slowed down or partially shut to manage accretion build-up. Operators said this reduces effective kiln volume and pulls metallization down. The shift in-charge estimated, in a casual conversation, that kiln-related issues (accretion + refractory wear + feeder jamming) account for roughly 40–50% of downtime in the sponge unit. This is an operator estimate, not a logged figure.
02
Metallization in the 88–93% range. Daily lab reports I was shown indicated metallization targets of 88–91%, with 92–93% on good days. An engineer demonstrated how metallization dropped when kiln speed wasn't adjusted in proportion to feed rate.
03
Raw material variability. Even with premium sourcing, operators mentioned that coal ash content varies batch-to-batch, and iron ore quality fluctuates enough to disturb feed-rate stability and the reducing-atmosphere chemistry.
04
Reduction-zone temperature excursions. SCADA screens in the control room sometimes showed reduction-zone temperatures pushing above 1150 °C — a threshold operators try to stay under to prevent ring formation.
05
Magnetic separator overload. On one shift, material queued at the product separation area because the magnetic separator was overloaded with char — a downstream symptom of a particular kiln run.
06
ESP maintenance event. The Electrostatic Precipitator was running, but one section was visibly under maintenance during my visit — confirming pollution-control upkeep is a parallel operational concern.

Pareto reading
Accretion and raw material variation are the “vital few.” Focus engineering effort here before chasing the remaining items. The shift in-charge's casual ~40–50% estimate of kiln-related downtime aligns with what was visibly disrupting operations.

Fishbone (Ishikawa) breakdown of the root causes of kiln accretion, applying the 6M framework. Built from operator conversations and standard DRI process literature.

Material
Ash content variability in coal — high ash + low ash-fusion temperature → low-melting eutectics inside the kiln. Iron-ore gangue chemistry (SiO₂ / Al₂O₃) drives ring composition.
Method
Coal-to-iron ratio (FC/Fe) outside the 0.4–0.52 optimal window. Air injection profile not adjusted dynamically to feed quality changes.
Machine
Refractory wear shifts the kiln's effective inner diameter and heat transfer behaviour over time. Air-fan positions/flows degrade between maintenance windows.
Measurement
SCADA monitors shell temperature but cannot predict accretion. Metallization tested in lab — a lagging indicator. Gas analysis (CO/CO₂/H₂) is the closest leading indicator available.
Man (Operator)
Manual setpoint adjustments depend on operator experience. Reduction-zone temperature occasionally seen above 1150 °C — the threshold for ring formation risk.
Environment
Ambient temperature and humidity affect combustion air density and kiln draft, which subtly shift the temperature profile over the course of a day.

Improvement recommendations grouped by horizon. Numbers are framework expectations from DRI literature, not measured plant outcomes.

Immediate
Strengthen raw material QA at intake. Tighten coal ash and ash-fusion-temperature acceptance criteria. Even small reductions in input variability translate directly into kiln stability.
Immediate
Set a hard SCADA alarm at 1150 °C reduction-zone temperature. Currently treated as a guideline operators try to stay under — making it a hard alarm with logged occurrences would create a measurable accretion-risk indicator.
Short-term
Begin logging downtime by stage and cause-code. A simple shift-by-shift downtime register would convert the current operator estimate (~40–50% kiln-related) into a measurable Pareto baseline the team could improve against.
Medium-term
Pilot an ANN-based metallization & accretion predictor. Train a multi-layer-perceptron on existing SCADA history (feed rates, multi-point temperatures, gas composition) cross-referenced with lab metallization. Literature reports R > 0.95 for similar 100 TPD plants. Deploy as a soft-sensor for shift operators.
Long-term
CFD-based kiln temperature profile study — typically run with an academic partner (IIT/NIT). DRI literature on similar 100 TPD plants reports 3–5% metallization gains from optimised inclination/rotation/air-injection configuration.
Reflection
What this internship taught me
Operations is about constraints
A 100 TPD plant doesn't get bigger by improving 5 things 10% each. It gets bigger by fixing the kiln. Pareto and Theory of Constraints both point to the same answer: spend disproportionate effort on the dominant constraint, ignore the rest until that one is solved.
SCADA shows; it doesn't predict
The control-room screens display every parameter in real time, but accretion still “sneaks up.” The next frontier in DRI ops is predictive — ANN soft sensors + CFD-validated models. This is where MBA-trained operations managers add real value: bringing data-science thinking to a domain that has historically been operator-experience driven.
Operator conversations > spreadsheets
The ~40–50% kiln-downtime estimate didn't come from a report. It came from a shift in-charge over tea. The most valuable information in a plant lives in the heads of the people who run it. Structured downtime logging would surface this — but the first hour of any improvement project should be on the shop floor, not in the office.
A note on this study's scope
This was an observational MBA internship study, not a measured Six Sigma project. The numbers presented are either standard reference values for 100 TPD coal-based DRI plants (from process literature), or estimates given to me casually by the operations team. No proprietary plant data, SCADA exports, or internal reports are reproduced here. Any specific quantitative claim is labelled with its source.
